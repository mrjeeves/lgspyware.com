Ever connected an LG monitor to your PC? Check it now — Windows may have silently installed adware that's still there

Plug in an affected LG monitor and Windows installs LG's app in seconds. No prompt. No consent. Installed by SYSTEM. Under pressure from Microsoft, LG has now agreed to switch off the McAfee ads — but nothing about the install changed, and the app's listed permissions still reach contacts, logins, location, and transactions — from the company now facing Texas AG allegations that its TVs screenshot and sell viewing data. Unplug the monitor; it stays. Gamers Nexus: “DO NOT BUY.”

● Latest update · July 25, 2026

LG is switching off the ad. The silent install is still on.

Late on July 18 (21:47 UTC), Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney joined a user's thread about the pop-ups and tagged Microsoft's Windows chief on X. Pavan Davuluri, Microsoft's EVP of Windows and Devices, replied hours later — 05:04 UTC on July 19 — with “Thanks, Tim. The team is looking into it.” Four days after Sweeney's post, on July 22, Davuluri returned with the outcome: “…We've connected with the team at LG and as an immediate next step, they have agreed to disable the McAfee pop-up from their app. We appreciate LG working with us toward a shared goal of a better experience for our mutual customers. We will keep improving here with our ecosystem partners.” By July 24, The Register reported that, at the time of writing, references to McAfee had been removed from the app's Microsoft Store listing.

If you were already hit, the ad going quiet is not the software leaving. The app stays installed, the delivery packages stay armed, and the machine still re-arms if a listed panel is reconnected. Removal is here, and it now includes the Windows Home method we owed you.

What's happening

Connecting certain LG monitors to a Windows PC silently installs the LG Monitor App (LGElectronics.LGMonitorApp), with no installation dialog and no consent. The app's first visible act is a McAfee pop-up (a “30-day trial” / “Scam Detector” offer). Gamers Nexus saw the offer on 31 of 32 consecutive boots. Follow the offer and, as Windows Latest documented, the free trial rolls into McAfee Plus Premium at $39.99 for the first year — in smaller text. That is the business model this monitor was quietly plugged into.

It arrives through Windows Update. Two LG packages, signed as drivers but carrying no driver code, are delivered for the connected monitor; one instructs Windows to fetch the app from the Microsoft Store, which the SYSTEM account then installs. We recovered that chain from one of our own machines, documented to the second. The detail is below, and the full log is in our forensic report.

It installs the moment you connect. And Windows keeps it alive after the monitor is gone.

We pulled the full install chain off one of our own machines, down to the second:

  • Silent at connection. September 30, 2025, 9:52 PM: Windows Update delivers two LG "driver" packages for the connected UltraGear. Neither contains driver code. One exists to fabricate a phantom child device. The other carries a single instruction: fetch LGElectronics.LGMonitorApp from the Microsoft Store. Half a minute later the app is installed by SYSTEM and registered into the user account at next logon. No prompt appears at any step.
  • The monitor left in October 2025. The app never did. Nine months after the panel was unplugged, the app self-updated at 2:11 AM on July 9, 2026, staged by SYSTEM, and the Store's install service touched it again on the morning of July 17. Windows was still re-staging the dead monitor's metadata as late as July 15.
  • Uninstalling the app alone leaves the machine armed. Both LG delivery packages stay in the Windows driver store, wired to 51 LG hardware IDs across 19 monitor models. Connect any of them and the whole chain fires again. The full removal is in the fix.

The minute-by-minute evidence, including LG's own INF directives, is in our forensic install report.

We are not the only ones seeing this. Windows Latest documented an owner of an LG 34GX900A-B who had the monitor for nearly three months and got the pop-up on a random login with no cable connected between the monitor and the PC at all — independent corroboration that once the software is on the machine, the panel's presence stops mattering.

What LG says, and what our machine shows

On July 22, LG emailed a statement to outlets that had asked for comment — Windows Latest, The Register, TechRadar and PCGamesN each report receiving it. There was no press release. It is worth reading closely, because it is precise about a charge nobody made and silent on the one that matters. Below, LG's statement as reported, set against what we recovered from our own machine. We quote LG's paragraph in full across the three rows, including its strongest denial, so you can judge it yourself.

LG says

“LG Electronics reiterates that McAfee is not installed automatically and is never installed without the user's explicit consent… McAfee will be installed only if a user actively chooses to proceed with the installation and provides consent. Under no circumstances is McAfee installed automatically or without user authorization.”

What we found

We agree, and we never said otherwise. McAfee was never the thing that installed silently. LG's own app was. The McAfee offer is what that app then put on screen, on 31 of 32 consecutive boots in Gamers Nexus's testing. Every sentence here is about McAfee. Not one is about the app that arrived uninvited to show it.

LG says

“The LG Monitor App Installer is distributed through Microsoft's official Windows distribution process, which included McAfee as an option.”

What we found

Correct, and that is the finding. Our chain shows exactly that route: Windows Update delivers two WHQL-signed LG packages, Microsoft co-signs them, and SYSTEM installs the app 32 seconds later with no prompt, no EULA, and no entry in Programs and Features. “Official” describes the road. It does not describe consent.

LG says

The Monitor App Installer “does not access, collect, or transmit any customer personal data.”

What we found

We have no evidence of data being exfiltrated, and we have never claimed any — our findings are about consent and persistence. But this sits against the permissions the app is listed as holding. It is also the one claim here that a packet capture can settle outright. If you can capture the app's traffic, send it.

What no one has said. Not LG, not Microsoft: that the app will stop installing itself. This is not our inference — it is a documented non-answer, twice. Gizmodo reported that it “reached out to LG again to ask if it still plans to install the LG Monitor App Installer without users' consent,” and published without a reply. The Register put the equivalent question to Microsoft and had no response by publication. The agreement announced on July 22 covers the pop-up. The mechanism underneath it is exactly as it was on July 17, and it is not LG-specific — it is a Windows feature any hardware vendor can use.

And it was never only McAfee. Gizmodo reports the app also served pop-ups for LG's own streaming service. Switching off one advertiser is not switching off advertising, which is worth remembering when reading “they have agreed to disable the McAfee pop-up.”

For contrast, Dell contacted TechSpot on July 22 to say that current versions of Alienware Command Center require users to approve installation and do not display McAfee or other third-party advertisements; Dell's documentation confirms Windows can initiate the AWCC install after detecting supported hardware, but says an opt-in prompt was added with AWCC 6.6 in February 2025. TechSpot revised its headline and article to draw that distinction. Same delivery road, a consent step bolted on. That is the difference between a companion app and this.

Two years quiet. Then the switch flipped.

The install base built silently long before the ads did. On Microsoft's own Tech Community, the complaint thread about this app opens on November 18, 2024, and for twenty months every post is about the mystery install: a July 2025 repair tech calling it a force-installed PUP on client machines, a March 2026 user documenting the install landing despite having both the device-metadata policy and the Store-disable policy enabled. Not one post mentions advertising. The community reply marked as the official solution told everyone the app was safe.

Then on July 1, 2026, the first ad report appears: a McAfee Scam Detector pop-up on a machine that had carried the app quietly. A second user reports the McAfee notifications the same day, and every reply since is about the ads.

That date lines up with the app's own version chain. On our machine: build 1.2405.3001.0 installed September 2025, then 1.2602.502.0, then 1.2606.1601.0 arriving by SYSTEM self-update on July 9, 2026. The numbering reads like year-month builds, and a June 2026 build rolling out through staggered Store updates lands exactly on the window where the thread turns into ad complaints, Reddit lights up, and Gamers Nexus's three-year-old UltraFine starts popping during production of their video.

Update, July 25: the June-build reading now has support from LG's own paperwork. On July 21, Windows Latest reported that the app's Microsoft Store listing carried a changelog entry reading “Additional App / – McAfee”, added in a recent update, with 30-day-free-trial copy and the line “Go to McAfee to install and get started.” A changelog entry is a shipped-in-the-build artifact, not a server-side flip. Note what it does not establish: that wording describes a directed install the user has to act on, which is consistent with LG's consent claim rather than a contradiction of it. We cite it for the build timing, not as a gotcha. Those references have since been removed from the listing.

What we still can't prove is whether the advertising is only in the build or can also be switched on remotely, since the app is internet-connected and its payload can change. Reader data can settle it: see what to send us.

Are you affected?

If an affected LG monitor has ever been connected to your Windows PC, even months ago, even if it's long gone, check your machine. The install needs no consent, and it doesn't leave when the monitor does, so “I unplugged it” is not the same as “it's clean.”

The 30-second check (no monitor required):

  1. Open Settings → Apps → Installed apps (Windows 10: Apps & features).
  2. Search LG Monitor and McAfee.
  3. See LG Monitor App or LG Monitor App Installer, or a McAfee entry you never installed? You were hit.

A second check, and it's the damning one. Open Reliability Monitor (press Win+R, run perfmon /rel) and look down the install history for 9PM9N6F47JB8-LGElectronics.LGMonitorApp. Windows Latest documented what you'll find next to it: Windows files the arrival not as an app install but as a “Successful Windows Update.” That is the whole problem in one log line — the thing that put advertising on your machine is recorded as routine maintenance.

Were you even eligible? Per Microsoft's documentation, cited by The Register and Windows Latest, this delivery needs three things to be true: you opted into “Recommended Settings” during Windows setup, you're signed in to the Microsoft Store, and you're online. Those are the default choices on a normal consumer install, which is why so many people qualify without ever making a decision — but if you declined Recommended Settings, that is a real reason you may have been spared.

The deep check, for techs: run pnputil /enum-drivers and look for lgmonitorappextension.inf or lgmonitorappsoftwarecomponent.inf from LG Electronics Inc. A machine can be armed with these packages even when the app itself is absent.

We're documenting machines that had an affected LG display connected in the past and still carry the app today. Found it on a PC whose LG monitor has long since been unplugged? Send us the app's install date and a screenshot, that evidence is exactly what we're collecting.

What it takes

Reading the app's Microsoft Store listing and LG's terms on air, Gamers Nexus found the auto-installed monitor app “technically” holds permission to use all system resources, plus:

System resources Internet connectivity Location Device data Contacts Credentials Transactions Online activity

It's a monitor. It should not want your logins, location, contacts, or transactions. This list is Gamers Nexus's reading, not our own legal analysis — we have not audited LG's agreements ourselves, and GN's own hedge was that the app “technically” holds these permissions. GN has since put lawyers on roughly 40,000 words of LG agreements; that work is still running.

LG's position, and ours: LG's July 22 statement ends “The LG Monitor App Installer does not access, collect, or transmit any customer personal data.” We are reporting that claim, not endorsing it — and we hold to our own limit here too: what is documented is a permission entitlement, not observed transmission. We have no capture showing this app sending anything. The gap between what a program reserves the right to take and what it demonstrably takes is real, and we won't paper over it in either direction. A packet capture closes it.

This isn't LG's first time

"Spyware" isn't hyperbole with LG. It's a pattern. The monitor is just the newest vector.

  • 2013: an LG TV was caught sending the filenames on a user's USB drive back to LG, and it kept transmitting after he opted out. The BBC reported it. LG admitted it.
  • Every 500ms: Texas AG Ken Paxton alleges LG TVs screenshot your screen twice a second, transmit it without consent, and sell the profile. That case is why the “wiretapping” pop-ups exist.
  • 2024–2025: ads shoved into TV screensavers, a force-installed and unremovable Copilot, and at least eight class actions in a decade.
  • Read the terms: LG makes you warn your house guests their voices “may be captured,” to comply with wiretapping laws.

LG's answer, in its own words. The same July 22 statement carried a second paragraph, published in full by TechRadar: “LG Electronics (LGE) reaffirms that LG TVs do not collect, record, or store ambient conversations. Voice recognition is an optional, user-initiated feature… Customers can use Smart TV features without enabling voice recognition. LGE is committed to protecting customer privacy…” We publish that in full because this section makes serious allegations and LG is entitled to its response beside them. Note what it denies — ambient audio — and what the Texas case actually alleges, which is periodic screenshotting of what's on screen.

Same company, now on your PC through the monitor. It never asked. Seen it phone home? Send the capture.

The fix

Two layers: remove the app, then remove LG's delivery packages so the machine can't re-arm. Uninstalling the app alone leaves both LG packages in the Windows driver store, and connecting any of 51 LG hardware IDs fires the whole chain again.

1. Remove the app. Settings → Apps → Installed apps, uninstall LG Monitor App (some versions list it as LG Monitor App Installer; the device tree calls it "LG Monitor Support Application"). Or from an elevated PowerShell:

Get-AppxPackage -AllUsers LGElectronics.LGMonitorApp | Remove-AppxPackage -AllUsers

2. Remove LG's two delivery packages. From an elevated prompt, list the driver store:

pnputil /enum-drivers

Find the two entries from provider LG Electronics Inc. with original names lgmonitorappextension.inf and lgmonitorappsoftwarecomponent.inf. Note each one's published name (oemXX.inf; the numbers differ on every machine, so read yours off the list). Then, once per package:

pnputil /delete-driver oemXX.inf /uninstall

This is surgical. It deletes LG's app-delivery packages and touches nothing else. Printers keep their setup and scanning software, mice and keyboards keep theirs, every other device on the machine is unaffected, and real driver updates keep flowing. Windows Update can re-offer LG's packages if a listed LG panel is connected again; we're monitoring for that on our own machines, and anything that changes gets reported here first.

Does the McAfee news change any of this? No. Disabling the pop-up removes the symptom that made people look. It does not uninstall the app, it does not remove either delivery package, and it does not close the consent gap. Everything above still applies.

Windows Home: the registry method

We said this was coming, so here it is — with its limits stated up front, because we went and read Microsoft's documentation rather than the summaries of it. The setting is the registry value behind the Group Policy “Prevent automatic download of applications associated with device metadata” (older ADMX files call the same setting at the same path “Prevent device metadata retrieval from the Internet” — search for both). From an elevated Command Prompt or PowerShell:

reg add "HKLM\SOFTWARE\Policies\Microsoft\Windows\Device Metadata" /v PreventDeviceMetadataFromNetwork /t REG_DWORD /d 1 /f

Reboot afterwards. To reverse it later, reg delete the same value. There is also a UI equivalent that works on Home with no registry editing at all: Settings → System → About → Advanced system settings → Hardware tab → Device Installation Settings → No.

Read this before you rely on it. We checked Microsoft's own documentation, and it does not promise what the internet says it promises.

  • What Microsoft documents it as blocking: the download of “applications associated with device metadata.” That is one specific delivery mechanism. Microsoft nowhere documents this setting as gating driver delivery — so the two WHQL-signed LG packages can still arrive through Windows Update with this set. Our forensic chain describes an INF-directive install path, and we cannot tell you this policy stops it.
  • Editions: Microsoft's policy documentation lists Pro, Enterprise, Education and IoT Enterprise. “Home” appears nowhere in it. The registry value is the same one the policy writes, and setting it on Home is harmless, but we will not tell you Microsoft supports it there, because Microsoft doesn't say so.
  • It's deprecated. Microsoft has marked device metadata as deprecated and slated for removal, replaced by Driver Package Container Metadata. A fix built on it has a shelf life.
  • The cost: companion software for every vendor's hardware stops arriving automatically, printers included. Actual drivers keep flowing.
  • The field evidence against it: a March 2026 user documented the install landing with the equivalent policy enabled.

So why publish it? Because we promised it, because it is real hardening against the device-metadata path, and because you deserve the caveats more than you deserve the confidence. We have not reproduced a blocked install with it on our own hardware. When we do, the result gets published here whichever way it goes. Removing the two packages remains the primary fix — it is the only step we have actually verified.

About the Group Policy options: community testing and press coverage verify that enabling "Prevent automatic download of applications associated with device metadata" (gpedit.msc → Computer Configuration → Administrative Templates → System → Device Installation) stops these installs on many machines, and it's a reasonable hardening step for techs, IT, and shops. Know two things before relying on it. First, the cost: companion software for every vendor's hardware stops arriving automatically, printers included, and you install vendor apps manually from then on. Drivers themselves keep flowing. Second, the reports conflict: a March 2026 user on Microsoft's own forums documented the install landing with that policy and the Store-disable policy both enabled, and our forensic chain explains how, since the documented delivery rode the Windows Update driver channel, which those policies don't gate. That conflict is exactly why the package removal above is our primary fix. The one policy that gates the driver channel outright also stops real driver updates, so we don't recommend it on home machines. Windows Home has no Group Policy Editor, and the registry equivalent is published above.
